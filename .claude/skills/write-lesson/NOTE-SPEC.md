# How a lesson is written

The production standard for a study lesson. Read it before writing or editing
one.

Every rule here came from a real defect in real material, which is why some of
them are oddly specific. Where a rule looks fussy, it is because the obvious way
of writing produced something that failed in use.

---

## A. Sourcing

1. **Read both sources in full before writing**: the slide deck and the
   transcript. Not excerpts, and not one of them.
2. **The two sources do different jobs.** The transcript carries mechanism,
   reasoning, causal direction, and the asides that explain why something
   matters. The slides carry structure, anatomy, figures, citations and learning
   outcomes. Written from one alone, a lesson is missing either the "why" or the
   "what".
3. **Confirm what you are looking at before you write about it.** Numbering in
   lecture material is frequently wrong: a deck's footers may name a different
   topic from its title, and an export may misnumber a part. Never trust a single
   location in a deck.
4. **Reorganise freely.** Source order is often not explanatory order. Where the
   source runs two things out of sequence, or buries the load-bearing claim on a
   wordless diagram, restructure so the argument reads forward.
5. **Lead with what the material is actually for.** Open by naming the single job
   this piece of the syllabus does. If the source never states it, state it
   anyway.
6. 🔴 **When a transcript is damaged, demote it to a check on the slides rather
   than treating the two as equal**, and say which source the lesson was written
   from. Rule 2 assumes both are intact. Where a transcript drops text
   mid-sentence, take every claim from the slides and use whatever the transcript
   preserved as confirmation.
7. 🔴 **Every clause about a study's design, sample or procedure traces to a
   sentence you have read.** If the source says *analysed*, write analysed, not
   *completed*: those are different numbers in any trial with dropout. If the
   design is not stated in what you read, do not name it. **This is the one error
   nothing downstream catches**, because the checker verifies identifiers and not
   whether the sentence around them is true, so an invented study design ships
   with a correct citation beneath it and reads perfectly.
8. 🔴 **Fetch the paper for any number or direction of effect you transmit.** The
   teaching material is a claim *about* the paper; it is not the paper. Decks
   routinely print a point estimate without its interval, take an effect size
   from the weakest subset of a literature, or keep the significant half of a
   result and drop the rest. At worst they state the **opposite** of the paper
   printed on the same slide. **A lesson that inverts a finding is more
   convincing than one that fumbles it**, so check the direction against the
   abstract before writing it in your own words.
9. **Text extraction and OCR fail in opposite directions, so use each for its own
   job.** Extraction silently drops whole sentences; OCR misreads digits. **Read
   the rendered page for anything numeric**, and use OCR for prose where
   extraction is unreliable. Never the reverse.

## B. Voice: a lesson stands alone

A lesson is an independent reference. A reader should be able to use it without
knowing it came from a taught course, and without meeting anyone.

### Never

- **Name a lecturer, presenter or interviewer.** No "Dr X explains", no "in
  conversation with", no attributing a quote to a person.
- **Refer to the delivery format.** No "the lecture", "this video", "the deck",
  "the slides", "the transcript", "slide 17", "the summary slide".
- **Narrate the source's behaviour.** No "the deck never says this plainly", "the
  slides run these out of order", "a long time is spent on this".
- **Reproduce interview scaffolding.** A question asked by a second speaker
  becomes a direct statement, or goes.

### Instead

**Emphasis is preserved; attribution is not.** If the source stresses something,
carry the stress: bold it, give it a callout, make it a pull quote with no
attribution line, or simply assert it. What is lost is only *who* said it.

| Instead of | Write |
| --- | --- |
| "Davies is making a methodological point before any content arrives." | "This is a methodological point, and it belongs before any content." |
| "Harrison asks whether there is a future in a clear-cut biomarker." | "Is there a future in a clear-cut biomarker? No." |
| "His phrase: the hope was for 'the wound that would account for depression'." | A pull quote carrying the phrase, with no cite line. |
| "The transcript carries the explanation; the slide does not." | Just give the explanation. |
| "Slide 14 plots symptom clusters against two axes." | "Symptom clusters are plotted against two axes." |
| "Summary of part 4" (as a heading) | "Core claims" |

### Transmit, never point

🔴 **The lesson must be readable with nothing else open.** Never gesture at
something the reader cannot see. "Notice the shape of this slide", "as shown in
the figure opposite", "the table lists six", "this diagram" all fail, not because
they name the format but because they **substitute a pointer for the content**.

The fix is never to delete the pointer and leave a gap. Carry the thing across:

| Pointer | Transmitted |
| --- | --- |
| "Notice the shape of this slide. Three of its four claims say what depression genetics is *not*." | "Notice the shape of the account: three of its four claims say what depression genetics is **not**." |
| "The table lists six deficiencies." | Give the six. |
| "The figure shows the two states." | Draw it, or describe both states in prose. |
| "See appendix 6 for further details." | Include the detail, or drop the sentence. A reference to an appendix the reader does not have is worthless. |

Anything the source conveyed through a visual, a layout or an adjacency has to be
re-expressed as words or as a diagram in the lesson itself. If a claim only made
sense because of where it sat on a page, restate the claim.

### Keep the insight, drop the frame

**This is the failure mode to watch for.** Removing "the deck never says this
plainly" must not remove the observation attached to it. The frame goes; the
point stays, asserted directly.

| Attributed original | Wrong fix | Right fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Part 1 does one job, and it is worth naming up front, because the deck never says it plainly: it establishes that mood is an actively regulated variable." | Delete the sentence. | "Part 1 does one job: it establishes that mood is an actively regulated variable." |
| "Slide 17 is a figure with no text, and it is the payoff of the whole part." | Delete. | "This interaction is the payoff of the whole part." |
| "The deck's summary undersells the part badly." | Delete. | Keep a Core claims section and add what it omits. |

**The test: if a reader would be worse informed after the edit, the edit was
wrong.** Anonymising is a change of voice, never a loss of content. Figure
captions in particular keep their job, which is to say what to take from the
picture: that is a point about the diagram, not about the source.

### What may stay

- The part's own id and the module code. These are syllabus structure and
  navigation keys, and they do not imply lectures.
- "Part N of M" as a document series marker.

## B2. Density: comprehensive and concise are both required

**The rule most likely to be violated by writing that feels good.**

The target loses **no information, no sharp formulation and no useful lens**,
while carrying **no narrative and no sentence read twice**. Somebody who wanted
the narrative would watch the lecture. Length is not evidence of thoroughness; it
is usually evidence of unedited first-draft prose.

### The worked example

Roughly 90 words, as first written:

> Notice the shape of this slide. Three of its four claims say what depression
> genetics is *not*. [four bullets] "Depression arises more from environmental
> than from genetic roots, with a complex and poorly understood set of
> interactions between the two." Harrison asks whether there is a future in a
> clear-cut biomarker. The answer is no. Davies's own formulation is the one
> worth remembering: genetics is not the big actor but has a strong supporting
> role, "It's not the star. In fact, there are no stars."

Roughly 25 words, as it should have been:

> **Depression arises more from environmental than from genetic roots, with a
> complex and poorly understood set of interactions between the two.** Genetics
> plays a supporting rather than a primary role, and there is no clear-cut
> biomarker.

Nothing was lost. The quote survives, the lens survives. What went is the setup,
the second speaker, the attribution, a restatement the next section covers
properly, and the announcement that a point is worth remembering instead of just
making it well.

### Rules

1. **Assert, do not recount.** No "then the deflation arrived", no "the argument
   runs from cost", no "take the origins first". State the thing.
2. 🔴 **Say each thing once, and look for the duplicate FIRST.** Before writing a
   claim, check whether a later section covers it properly; if so, cut it here.
   Whole duplicated blocks are worth more than every sentence-level trim put
   together, and a duplicate that drifts out of step with the passage it repeats
   is how a lesson comes to contradict itself.
3. **No sentence whose only job is to introduce the next sentence.** Delete
   signposting: "which produces the question", "this is worth stating slowly",
   "two things fall out of this".
4. **No praising the material.** "The most mechanically satisfying passage",
   "worth memorising" are verdicts the reader can form. If something is
   important, make it prominent typographically; do not announce it.
5. **Keep a quote only where the wording carries something a paraphrase would
   lose.** "There are no stars" earns its place. Most do not.
6. **Prefer a table or a list to a paragraph** whenever the content has repeated
   fields or parallel items. Prose about four studies is four table rows.
7. **Cut every hedge that adds nothing**: "it is worth noting that",
   "interestingly", "as we have seen", "of course".
8. **A callout must earn its box.** One per section at most, for the thing a
   careful reader would still miss.

### The test before publishing

Read each paragraph and ask: **what would a reader lose if this were deleted?**
If the answer is "nothing they will not get two sections later", delete it. If it
is a fact, a distinction, a formulation or a connection, keep it, then consider
whether it can be said in half the words.

## B3. Highlightable: the sentence is the unit that leaves the lesson

**This one has a mechanical reason, not a stylistic one.**

A lesson is not only read. It is **harvested.** The reader highlights as they go,
and short highlights become flashcard terms. **The sentence is therefore the
export unit**, and prose that mixes scaffolding into a claim cannot be
highlighted cleanly: they either drag the scaffolding along or make a fiddly
part-sentence selection, and what lands is wrong either way.

So this is not "that reads better". It is: **a sentence that cannot be
highlighted whole is a defect**, however good it sounds.

### The worked example

What shipped:

> The amygdala has been mentioned in passing throughout the topic. Here it gets
> its role: the initial recognition and processing of external stimuli,
> particularly emotional ones. Whatever it passes upstream has already been
> tagged for emotional significance.

What it should have been:

> **The amygdala's role:** the initial recognition and processing of external
> stimuli, particularly emotional ones. Whatever it passes upstream has already
> been tagged for emotional significance.

What went is a sentence about the *document* and a stage direction. Both describe
the reader's journey rather than the amygdala, and both sit in front of the
claim, so any highlight starting at the top of the paragraph drags them along.

### The distinction this adds

Section B forbids referring to the **source**. This forbids referring to the
**lesson**: where you are in it, what it has covered, what it is about to do.
They are different failures, and the second is easier to miss because nothing in
the sentence sounds like a lecture.

| Talking about the lesson | What it should be |
| --- | --- |
| "X has been mentioned in passing. Here it gets its role: …" | "**X's role:** …" |
| "This is the conceptual core of Part 1" | Delete, or make the claim itself prominent |
| "It is worth naming up front, because …" | Name it |
| "Two things fall out of this" | State both things |
| "Do not treat these as two topics" | "Kindling as an epidemiological pattern and kindling as a mechanism are one phenomenon described at two levels" |

🔴 **The last row is the important one. This is not a licence to delete
guidance.** A warning about a real confusion is content and stays. What changes is
that it becomes a claim about the material rather than an instruction about how to
read the page.

### Rules

1. **No sentence whose subject is the lesson.** If the grammatical subject is
   "this section", "this part", "the topic" or an implied "we", rewrite so the
   subject is the thing being described.
2. **No positional deictics**: "here", "above", "below", "so far", "now", "at
   this point", "finally", when they mean a place in the document rather than a
   place in a mechanism.
3. **Use a label, not a run-up.** A bolded noun-phrase and a colon does in three
   words what a sentence of narration does in twenty: `**The amygdala's role:**`.
4. **Front-load the subject**, so a highlight starting at the sentence start is
   already useful.
5. **A sentence should survive out of context.** Avoid opening on a pronoun or a
   "this" that only resolves from the sentence before.

### A cross-reference is a citation, not a subject

**A pointer to another part is content and stays.** Knowing that a phenomenon is
established in one place and mechanised in another is a real fact about the
material. What changes is the **grammatical position**: the part goes in a
parenthesis, not the subject slot.

| Document as subject | Part as citation |
| --- | --- |
| "Part 2 gave you kindling as an epidemiological pattern… Part 4 gives the same phenomenon a mechanism." | "**Kindling, epidemiologically** (Part 2): … **Mechanistically:** …" |
| "Part 1 built the healthy regulator. Part 2 asks where the fault comes from." | "The healthy regulator (Part 1) has a fault, and its sources are social, environmental and psychological." |

### The test before publishing

For each sentence carrying a claim: **could it be dragged over and pasted into a
flashcard with no edit?** If not, because the first clause is about the document,
that clause is the defect.

A faster second test: **read only the bold and the first four words of each
sentence.** If that skim does not carry the argument, the sentences start in the
wrong place.

🔴 **Run this scan halfway through a topic, not only at the end.** A scan run once
at the end measures a draft; a scan run in the middle changes the one still being
written. Topics whose early parts were scanned before the later parts were written
have come back clean on the first build; topics scanned only at the end have not.

## C. Structure

Not a rigid template, but these earn their place and their absence should be
deliberate:

1. **Masthead**: title, and a standfirst naming the single job this part does and
   the claim it turns on.
2. **Numbered sections**, in explanatory order. Numbering is justified when the
   material is a genuine sequence of argument, and not otherwise.
3. **Callouts** for the thing a reader would otherwise miss: the buried
   distinction, the finding that reframes the rest, the trap.
4. **Diagrams** for mechanisms (see E).
5. **Tables** for evidence trajectories, comparisons and mappings.
6. **Core claims**: the source's own summary, reproduced faithfully and
   de-attributed.
7. **Terms to be able to define cold**: a glossary. The highest-value section per
   line for revision.
8. **References** (see D).

🔴 **An anatomical entry names what the structure does.** Where a glossary term
is a brain structure, a nucleus, a tract or a named network, its definition
states **the functions it is implicated in**, not only where it sits and what it
borders. A definition that gives a reader only a location tells them nothing they
can revise from.

**This section is an ingestion source, not only a revision aid**, which is why
this is a rule rather than a preference. `server/glossary_extend.py` harvests
every `<dt>`/`<dd>` pair straight out of it into the course glossary, and that
glossary is what the reader's lookup popover shows when the term is selected
**anywhere in the course**. A thin definition does not stay in the lesson that
wrote it.

🔴 **Flag, do not silently fix.** Where the source is internally inconsistent,
looks factually doubtful, or contradicts another part, say so and mark it
unresolved. Never overrule the source silently, and never assert a correction as
fact without evidence. Give the reader what to check.

**Cross-link across parts.** Where a part completes, contradicts or reframes an
earlier one, say so. Much of the value is in the joins.

**Three things a lesson does when the source is wrong.** The source being wrong
is normal rather than exceptional. None of these replaces the prose; they sit
alongside it.

1. **A contradiction gets a call-out that quotes both sides.** Where the teaching
   material disagrees with the paper it cites, a small box says what the material
   says and what the paper says, in their own terms. **It does not adjudicate.**
   The reader sees the disagreement rather than being told who won.
   🔴 **This box is a deliberate, narrow exception to section B**, which
   otherwise forbids referring to the source at all: the box requires saying
   what the source claims, and inside the box that later, more specific rule
   wins. The rules for staying inside it: say **"the course material"**, never
   "the lecture", "the deck", "the slides" or "the transcript"; never name a
   person; and never use the box to comment on how the material is put
   together. Everywhere outside the box, section B is unchanged.
2. **Stripped statistics come back in a collapsed section.** The body keeps its
   readable sentence; a folded section beneath it carries the intervals, the
   sample sizes and the non-significant arms the source dropped. It folds because
   a reader revising does not want the interval every time and a reader checking
   a claim always does.

   The markup is `<details class="deets"><summary>The study, in
   detail</summary>...</details>`. 🔴 **Fold detail that SUPPORTS an argument,
   never detail that IS one.** A study's arms, sample sizes, instruments and
   durations support a claim the prose has already made, so they fold. A table
   whose whole purpose is to show something the source hid does not: folding it
   re-hides the thing. The test is whether the reader loses the point by leaving
   it shut. Wrapping a table cannot move a highlight's anchor, because table
   cells and `<summary>` are not blocks, but verify that rather than trust it.
3. **A corrected or retracted paper keeps its place and gains a marker.** A small
   caution sign beside the citation links to that paper's reference entry, and
   that entry says it was corrected or retracted **and links the notice itself.**
   Every one, not only the ones where the correction changes the claim: the noise
   is handled by keeping the marker small inline and the detail at the end.

## D. Citations and links

### Coverage must be exhaustive

1. **Every paper, study or author-year mentioned anywhere in the part gets
   captured.** Not a selection.
2. 🔴 **Sweep the slide-corner citation clusters.** Most citations in lecture
   material are not in the body text. They sit in small print in a corner, as a
   run-on string like `Billman 2020; McEwen & Akil 2020; Sterling 2014`, attached
   to a slide title. **These are the easiest thing in the whole process to
   miss**, because OCR interleaves them with unrelated text and they read as
   noise. Before writing the reference section, re-scan the slide text hunting
   specifically for `Author Year` and `Author & Author Year`, and reconcile the
   total against the body.
3. **Also sweep the transcript**, which sometimes names a study the slides do not.
4. **Reconcile the two lists**, and note any citation appearing in one but not
   the other.

### Links must be verified

5. **Resolve a real DOI for each reference and verify it.** Confirm author, year
   AND title match what the source cited, then link `https://doi.org/<doi>`.
6. 🔴 **Never fabricate or reconstruct an identifier.** A wrong one is worse than
   no link, because it sends the reader silently to the wrong paper. The
   signature of a constructed identifier is being a few digits from the right one.
7. **Where a DOI cannot be verified**, say so on that entry and give a lookup
   link instead, explicitly marked unverified. Never mix verified and unverified
   links without labelling which is which.
8. **Not everything has a DOI.** Books, society reports and web resources get
   their canonical URL, with the type noted.

### Corrections, and the checker that finds them

**Check for a published correction against more than one index.** Neither of the
main ones is a superset of the other: a paper's corrigendum can be recorded by
one and entirely absent from the other, and both directions occur. Checking a
single index and concluding "no correction" is not a check.

🔴 **A check that fails must fail for the RIGHT reason, and a wrong check is
fixed rather than suppressed.** When the verifier fires on something you are
confident is correct, **the first hypothesis is that the check is wrong**. Adding
an identifier to a skip list is allowed only once you can state what the check
should have done instead. The failure this prevents is invisible: a checker that
cries wolf trains you to silence it, and the thing it eventually silences is the
one that mattered. A real instance: a defect made **every lesson citing a
retraction notice fail for a spurious reason**, and the tempting fix would have
hidden the retraction.

### How citations actually go wrong

🔴 **Every one of these produced a working link to the wrong document.** "The link
resolves" proves nothing; all of them return HTTP 200.

- **A real identifier for the wrong work.** A bibliographic query for a paper
  returns a **post-publication review** of it, or an unrelated preprint. Read the
  returned record's *type* and *title*, not just that it resolved.
- 🔴 **An identifier written from memory.** The most confident kind, and the
  worst measured rate so far: in one batch of twenty-five remembered DOIs, all
  twenty-five resolved and **four were a different paper**, each a real,
  plausible, adjacent-field work returning HTTP 200. Memory produces valid
  identifiers, not correct ones. Compare the returned record's AUTHOR and TITLE
  against what you are citing, every time, however sure you are.
- **A search for a book returns a review of the book**, correctly titled after it,
  in a real journal, with a working identifier. A book gets no identifier unless
  the book itself carries one.
- **A surname matched against a bibliography instead of a paper against a claim.**
  The tell is a narration naming one author where the reference names several.
  The check that catches it is reading the cited paper's title against the claim
  it is attached to.
- **A reference textbook cited for a clinical trial**, with a journal identifier
  belonging to a review of the textbook. Internally consistent, entirely wrong.
- **OCR adjacency.** A floating attribution line beside a slide may belong to a
  different slide. Where a deck numbers its citations, resolve the number rather
  than believing the adjacency, and note that a flag raised on a floating line
  would be a false one, which is worse than missing it.
- **A list that marks its own entries "assumed" has told you it was not
  checked**, and every other entry inherits that. Treat it as a reason to verify
  the whole list.
- **Retractions.** Check for them, and **flag rather than drop**: a reader who
  meets the citation in the module's own list deserves the warning. Dropping it
  silently leaves them unarmed.
- 🔴 **Reuse is not verification.** Identifiers copied from an already-"verified"
  pool carry that pool's errors forward. Sweep every identifier in a lesson, not
  only the new ones.

### Placement

9. **Inline where relevant.** When a paper is discussed, argued with, or is the
   evidence for a claim, link it at that point. The reader should not scroll to
   the end to follow up a claim they are reading now.
10. **Complete list at the end regardless**, including the ones mentioned only in
    a slide corner.
11. **Group the end list by what each reference supports**, not alphabetically.
    Alphabetical order is useless when hunting for the evidence behind a claim.
12. **Say what each reference is for.** "The meta-analysis behind the
    no-major-changes claim" is worth more than a title.
13. **Flag reading-list overlap.** If a cited paper is on the module reading list,
    say so, and give its week.

### A study described in the body carries its reference at the point of description

**A trailing References list does not discharge rule 9.** A reader partway through a
section cannot tell which of thirty-odd references the paragraph in front of them is
describing, and "verified DOIs only" is easy to satisfy with a bibliography while every
study in the body goes unattributed.

**A reference tag is enough, and it does not have to read as prose.** A bracketed
author-year that links to the entry in the References list satisfies this in full. Write
the study's name where you describe it; author and year is enough, a linked tag is better,
and the DOI still goes in the References list, verified, exactly as before.

**This is a rule rather than a note because it was measured, and it was systematic.** In
one library of thirty lessons: 321 passages describing a study, 1,284 DOI links, and the
links were in the bibliographies. In the first lesson opened by hand, 11 of its 12 sections
described a study with no link inside them, while 36 DOIs sat at the end. The lesson
described a secondary analysis with 23 patients per arm across three named treatment arms
and named no study at all.

**A passage that describes a study with no source anywhere needs a source found, not a
tag.** Two lessons in that library carry study language and zero DOIs of any kind; a tag
cannot be written for a paper nobody has identified.

## E. Diagrams

1. **Draw mechanisms, not labels.** A diagram earns its place when it shows
   something a reader would otherwise assemble from prose: a flow, a loop, a
   two-state contrast, a trajectory.
2. **Draw what the source states but does not show.** The highest-value diagrams
   are reconstructions of a claim made in words with no figure.
3. **Label the arrows.** An unlabelled arrow means "related somehow".
4. **Colour carries meaning or nothing.** Pick one hue for regulated, healthy or
   active and one for dysregulated, broken or pathological, use them consistently
   across a series, and never decoratively.
5. **The caption states the claim**, and says what to take from the picture, not
   what is in it.
6. **Hand-authored inline SVG**, theme-aware through CSS variables, no libraries.
7. 🔴 **Look at the drawn result, not the markup. This is a required step.**
   Labels collide, arrows pass under text, and text clips at the edges of a
   viewBox. None of that is visible in the source, and roughly one figure in four
   has such a defect. Verify by screenshotting the rendered page in a browser.
8. 🔴 **The same applies to any page a tool rewrote.** A tool's own report of
   success is not evidence that it succeeded. If something reorganised, rotated
   or re-processed a page, look at the page: a real case had a tool rotate an
   upright page by 270 degrees and report it as fixed.

## F. What gets produced

**Two files per part, and the second one is not optional.**

| File | Purpose | Provenance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `courses/<CODE>/<DOC>-<slug>.html` | The lesson, in the content-only shape the reader composes around (see `SKILL.md`). Must stand alone. | **None** |
| `courses/<CODE>/<DOC>-outline.md` | The same content as a terse hierarchical outline, for grep and for pasting elsewhere. A working file, not a published resource. | **All of it**, in a block at the end |

The HTML carries **no provenance at all**: no source filenames, no file ids, no
slide ranges, no runtimes, no numbering warnings, no OCR complaints. It reads as
an independent resource end to end. The outline is where all of that goes, and
it may reference the source freely. Its body should still read cleanly, but it
is not held to Section B.

🔴 **The outline is load-bearing and its absence is silent, which is why it says
"not optional" above.** `course_text()` builds the digest that whole-course
questions are answered from, and it uses **1500 characters of the outline per
lesson where one exists and falls back to 700 characters of the lesson's raw
opening where it does not**. A course written without outlines answers
whole-course questions from a thinner, uncurated digest and nothing anywhere
says so. The second loss is the audit trail: provenance, slide mapping and
numbering warnings deliberately live here rather than in the published lesson,
so for a course without outlines they live nowhere.

**This requirement was missing from this file until 2026-08-30** and one whole
course (38 lessons) was written without a single outline. `verify_course.py`
reports it now: `lesson outlines  partial (0 of 38)`.

Navigation quirks ("this deck's footers say Topic 4") are not provenance and
belong wherever the course's own notes live, where they are actionable.

## G. House style

- 🔴 **No em dashes, anywhere, ever.** Commas, parentheses, colons, or two
  sentences.
- British spelling, following the source material.
- Match the established design system exactly; a set of lessons must read as one
  series.
- Write for a postgraduate reader who is revising: not for a novice, and not for
  an examiner.

## The gate

```
python3 server/verify_notes.py --notes courses/<CODE>
```

Block numbering, every existing highlight still resolving, links, DOIs, house
style, and whether the JavaScript and CSS parse.

🔴 **A compliance scan is evidence only for the strings it contains.** A section
is "cleared" against a named scan, never in general. A scan built from lecturers'
*names* once passed a file that still said "the deck follows", "the deck's four
bullets" and "the lecturer's summary", because nobody had told it about the role
nouns. When you record something as cleared, record which scan cleared it.

🔴 **A run that finishes suspiciously fast has not done the work.** It checks DOIs
over the network. Read the counts it prints and confirm they are the number of
lessons you expect.
